Q1. In the following sentence, two words are given in bold. They may or may not be in their correct positions. Select the option that gives the correct interchange to make the sentence grammatically and contextually correct.
The government decided to **abolish** (A) the outdated law and **implement** (B) a modern framework.
(A) Both A and B are correct in their current positions
(B) Swap A and B
(C) Change 'abolish' to 'create'
(D) Change 'implement' to 'destroy'

Answer: A
Explanation: The sentence is logically sound. You "abolish" an outdated law and "implement" a modern framework. No swap is needed.

Q2. In the following sentence, two words are given in bold. Select the option that gives the correct interchange.
The sudden **prosperity** (A) of the banking sector led to widespread economic **collapse** (B).
(A) Swap A and B
(B) Change 'prosperity' to 'growth'
(C) Change 'collapse' to 'boom'
(D) Both A and B are correct

Answer: A
Explanation: Prosperity leading to collapse makes no logical sense. Swapping them makes it: "The sudden collapse of the banking sector led to widespread economic prosperity." Wait, that's still illogical. A collapse leading to prosperity?
Let's rewrite Q2 for a proper word swap context.
Corrected Q2: The doctor tried to **aggravate** (A) the patient's severe pain rather than **alleviate** (B) it.
(A) Swap A and B
(B) Both A and B are correct
(C) Change 'aggravate' to 'worsen'
(D) Change 'alleviate' to 'soothe'

Answer: A
Explanation: A doctor's duty is to alleviate (reduce) pain, not aggravate (worsen) it. Therefore, swapping A and B makes the sentence logically correct: "The doctor tried to alleviate the patient's severe pain rather than aggravate it."

Explanation: A drought would "deplete" reservoirs, not replenish them. Swapping A and B gives: "The relentless drought helped to deplete the city's rapidly shrinking reservoirs and completely replenish..." Wait, drought doesn't replenish anything.
Let's use a standard two-word swap.

If the original is: The lawyer tried to **condemn** (A) his client’s actions to the jury, hoping they would ultimately **justify** (B) him.
Swapping makes it: The lawyer tried to justify his client's actions... hoping they would ultimately condemn him. Still doesn't make sense (why would he hope they condemn him?).
